Building a personal cloud at home

How I replaced paid cloud storage with a self-hosted setup that costs less, does more, and taught me a lot along the way.

← Articles
12 May 2026 · — min read

For years I'd been paying a monthly fee for cloud storage I barely used. The numbers were small enough to ignore — until I added them up across a decade and realised I'd spent more than the cost of a small server.

This is the story of replacing that subscription with something I own. It cost a few weekends and one mistake involving a power supply, and it's been running quietly under my desk for the past four months.

Why bother

The honest answer is that I wanted to learn. The cloud providers do a fine job for most people, and I'm not going to claim my setup is more reliable than theirs. What it is, is mine — I know exactly what's running, where the data lives, and what happens when something breaks.

The second answer is cost. Over a long enough timeline, even modest subscriptions add up to real money. A used mini-PC, two hard drives, and a UPS came in under what I'd have paid the cloud over the next three years.

The hardware

The whole thing runs on a second-hand small-form-factor desktop with 16GB of RAM and two 4TB drives in a mirror. Nothing exotic. The trick was choosing parts that would run quietly and on little power — this machine sits in my study, not a data centre, and I didn't want it to sound like one.

  • An Intel N100 mini-PC for the brains (low power, plenty of cores)
  • Two 4TB drives mirrored with ZFS — one drive can fail without losing anything
  • A small UPS so a quick load-shedding cycle doesn't corrupt anything
  • A wired connection to the router; Wi-Fi was too flaky for backups

How it works now

The setup runs Nextcloud for file sync (a replacement for the paid service it displaced) and a handful of smaller tools alongside it: a photo backup target for my phone, a media server for the family, and a nightly off-site backup to an encrypted bucket I rent for next to nothing.

The most surprising thing has been how little maintenance it needs. After the initial month of fiddling, it's been quietly humming along, doing exactly what I asked it to.

If you're considering something similar, my one piece of advice is this: start small. Get one service working — file sync, say — and live with it for a month before adding the next. Most self-hosting horror stories start with someone trying to set up ten services on day one and burning out before any of them work properly.

What I'd do differently

I'd buy the UPS first, not last. I'd also pick a quieter case from the start; the one I have is fine but it spins up its fan more than I'd like when the drives are working hard.

Otherwise, no regrets. The thing works, the data is mine, and the monthly bill is gone.
